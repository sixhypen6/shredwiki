1. Blade Design

Blade design refers to the structural arrangement, geometry, and placement logic of cutter modules on a shredder. The foundational metrics of effective blade design are density, block efficiency, and total area coverage.

1.1 Modern Blades

Cross Blade configuration.

Modern blade architecture utilizes compressor block invincibility and outward compression settings to form a vertically and horizontally spaced cross blade. This represents the current general meta standard due to its high area coverage and block efficiency.

Common variants prune side geometry from four-point layouts down to three or two points. Flat (2-point) blades trade overall hit area for reduced motor load, but deliver lower hit-rate frequency compared to full cross blades.

1.2 Sinusoid & Spirals

Sinusoid / Spiral Blade.

Single-pointed blades (1-point) perform exceptionally poorly when used in isolation due to minimal sweep area and lack of rotational symmetry. To compensate, 1-point designs are constructed into continuous angular loops to form Sinusoids or Spirals.

While sinusoid patterns provide excellent physical spacing along the rotor axis, they utilize cutter blocks less efficiently than cross setups. A common design flaw is stacking these patterns into double-helix structures; this drastically over-allocates block count, causing high local density that triggers self-pingspiking.

1.3 Density & Efficiency

Balancing blade density is critical for avoiding collision-locking. Overly dense blade arrays increase local physical interactions, leading to severe ping degradation and reduced phase effectiveness.

2. Cutter Rules

Cutter blocks operate under specific engine limitations, involving both scripted combat rules and server physics checks.

2.1 Minimum Distance

Cutters enforce a minimum spatial clearance rule to prevent redundant overlapping and side-by-side cutter stacking.

2.2 Speed Limits & Ping

Cutters exhibit upper limits on operational speed. As rotational velocity increases, server-side and client-side hitboxes displace further out of sync. Because damage checks evaluate on the server, network latency (ping) directly dictates hit register accuracy. At extreme speeds exceeding thousands of Studs Per Second (SPS), cutter hitboxes fail to calculate entirely.

2.3 Damage Per Second & Cooldowns

Upon registering a hit, a cutter block enters a 0.1-second internal cooldown. This cooldown prevents a single cutter from dealing infinite frame-by-frame damage or instantly overloading the server thread.

Demean bypasses this limitation by forcing rotation to align precisely with server physics ticks (60Hz). By rotating the exact angular distance required to place a new cutter face into the identical spatial coordinates of the preceding cutter, the blade maintains uninterrupted maximum hits-per-second.

Example: A Flat Blade (15 hits/sec) achieves half the damage output of a 4-point Cross Blade (30 hits/sec) when running on 15π (47.124) Demean configurations.

2.4 Pingspiking

When cutter arrays register simultaneous hits across dense target assemblies, network packet processing can spike indefinitely. This desynchronizes client-to-server replication, leaving the shredder stationary on the server while mobile on the client—often leading to instantaneous destruction.

3. Demean & Cutter Enhancement (CE)

3.1 Core Functionality

Demean boosts blade damage and clipping capability by tuning MotorSpeed to match the server physics simulation rate. The Roblox physics solver runs at a maximum rate of 60Hz (60 updates per second).

To calculate the target motor speed for a 4-point cross blade, the following formula is applied:

(2π × 60) ÷ (360 ÷ 45) = 15π ≈ 47.124

For a 3-pointed "Tri-blade", the required speed is 20π (≈ 62.832). Since individual motor speeds cap at 50, achieving 20π requires a 2x Demean stack (two motors chained in series, set to 31.416 each).

  • Optimal MotorTorque: Extensively tested benchmark values identify 0.63 as the optimal torque threshold for Demean stability.
  • Rotational Direction: Spinning blades in reverse creates an inward vacuum effect that pulls enemy assemblies toward the core, whereas forward rotation deflects targets outward.
